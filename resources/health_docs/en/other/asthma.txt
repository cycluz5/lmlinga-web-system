Symptoms

Asthma is a long-term condition that affects the airways and may cause wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing, especially at night or in the early morning. Although asthma is always present, symptoms usually become worse during an asthma attack, which occurs when something irritates the lungs.

During an asthma attack, a person may experience coughing, wheezing, chest tightness, and difficulty breathing. The airways, which carry air to and from the lungs, become swollen and narrow. Thick mucus may also build up inside the airways, making it harder for air to flow in and out of the lungs.

Asthma can be managed by recognizing the early warning signs of an attack, avoiding known triggers, and following the treatment plan recommended by your doctor.

Diagnosis
Diagnosing asthma can be difficult, especially in children younger than five years old. A healthcare provider will evaluate your symptoms and medical history to determine whether you have asthma.
During the examination, the doctor may ask if you often cough, particularly at night, or if breathing problems become worse during exercise or at certain times of the year. You may also be asked about wheezing, chest tightness, long-lasting colds, and whether asthma, allergies, or other breathing conditions run in your family. The doctor may also ask about your home environment and whether asthma symptoms affect your school, work, or daily activities.

A lung function test called spirometry may also be performed. This test measures how much air you can breathe out after taking a deep breath, both before and after using asthma medication, to determine how well your lungs are working.

Symptom Management
To keep asthma under control, take your medications exactly as prescribed by your doctor and avoid anything that may trigger an asthma attack.

Asthma treatment varies from person to person, so not everyone uses the same medication. Some medicines are inhaled, while others are taken by mouth. Asthma medicines are generally classified as reliever medicines, which provide quick relief during attacks, and controller medicines, which help prevent symptoms and reduce the frequency of asthma attacks. If you find that you need your reliever medicine more often, consult your doctor because your treatment plan may need to be adjusted.

Like all medicines, asthma medications may cause side effects. Most side effects are mild and temporary. If you have concerns about your medication, talk to your doctor for guidance.
