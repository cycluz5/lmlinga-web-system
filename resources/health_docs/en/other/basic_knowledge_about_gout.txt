Basic Knowledge About Gout

Gout is a form of arthritis that usually affects one joint at a time and can cause intense pain. It develops when there is too much uric acid in the body. Excess uric acid can form sharp crystals inside a joint, leading to inflammation, swelling, and severe discomfort.
Gout symptoms may appear and disappear over time. Periods without symptoms are common, but the condition can worsen if left untreated.

Gout Flares
A gout flare occurs when symptoms suddenly become severe. During a flare, a person may experience intense joint pain, swelling, redness, and warmth that can last for several days or even weeks.
After a flare, symptoms may disappear for a period of time, known as remission. However, without proper treatment, gout attacks may become more frequent, last longer, and increase the risk of joint damage. Hard lumps called tophi may also develop due to long-term high uric acid levels. These lumps can be painful and may permanently damage the affected joints.

Signs and Symptoms
People with gout commonly experience the following symptoms in the affected joint:
Severe pain
Swelling
Redness
Warmth around the joint
These symptoms occur because uric acid crystals build up inside the joint, causing inflammation and pain.

Risk Factors
Several factors can increase uric acid levels and raise the risk of developing gout. These include:
Being overweight or obese
Taking certain medications, such as diuretics (water pills)
Eating or drinking foods high in purines, which the body converts into uric acid, including:
Alcoholic beverages
Sugary drinks
Foods containing high-fructose corn syrup
Red meat and shellfish

Who Is More Likely to Develop Gout?
Although anyone can develop gout, it is more common among:
Men
People with a family history of gout
Individuals with certain medical conditions, such as:
Heart failure
High blood pressure
Insulin resistance
Diabetes
Kidney disease or reduced kidney function

Diagnosis
Early diagnosis is important because it allows treatment to begin before the condition becomes more serious.
Since gout symptoms are similar to those of other joint diseases, a healthcare provider should confirm the diagnosis. Gout is often easiest to diagnose during a flare because uric acid crystals are easier to detect in the affected joint. It is also important to remember that not everyone with high uric acid levels develops gout.
To diagnose gout, a doctor may perform:
A physical examination
X-ray imaging
Laboratory tests
A review of your medical history

Managing Gout
Treatment
Your healthcare provider may recommend medications to help control gout. These may include prescription or over-the-counter pain relievers, such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen, to reduce pain and swelling during a flare. Medicines that lower uric acid levels may also be prescribed to help prevent future attacks and reduce the formation of tophi.

Self-Care
Along with taking prescribed medicines, healthy lifestyle habits can help manage gout.
Regular physical activity, such as at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise each week, is recommended. It is also helpful to reduce foods and drinks that are high in purines, including alcoholic beverages (especially beer and hard liquor), sugary foods and drinks, red meat, organ meats, and certain seafood such as anchovies, sardines, mussels, scallops, trout, and tuna.
